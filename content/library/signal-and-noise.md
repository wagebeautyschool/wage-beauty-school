## The usual picture

The standard advice is to "filter out the noise" and "focus on the signal," as if the two arrived pre-labelled and the only task were sorting. In practice they do not. Noise is not the absence of signal. Noise is signal you have not yet learned to read, or signal on a channel you are not currently listening to.

## Three kinds of what we call noise

**Signal in the wrong units.** A market number, a mood in a room, a slightly-off sentence in an email — real information, arriving in a form you have not learned to decode. The fix is not to discard it. The fix is to learn the units.

**Signal at the wrong scale.** Day-to-day variation looks like chaos and month-to-month variation looks like a trend, and they are the same data. Much of what feels like noise is just the wrong zoom level. Change the window before you conclude the signal is not there.

**Actual noise.** Genuinely random variation with no information in it. This exists, and treating it as meaningful is how people find faces in clouds and patterns in coincidence. The skill is telling this apart from the first two — usually by asking whether the pattern survives a change of scale or a second sample.

## A filter that does not discard

The mistake is to build a filter that throws things away permanently. Better: build one that *routes*.

- Things you can read now → act on.
- Things that might be signal in unfamiliar units → set aside, labelled, to learn the units later.
- Things that repeat across scales and samples → promote; they are probably real.
- Things that appear once, at one scale, and never again → down-weight, but do not delete the record.

The difference matters because the expensive errors in both directions are symmetric. Acting on noise wastes effort. Discarding signal because it arrived badly formatted loses information you cannot get back.
