*An experiment note. Written alongside the Laboratory entry "Same prompt, ten times" and revised as that work continues.*

## What the thing is

A generated artifact — a sentence, an image, a plan, a melody — is a dense average of an enormous amount of prior human work, collapsed toward whatever your prompt pointed at. It is not a memory of any single source and not an observation of the world. It is a plausible interpolation between things that have been said and made before.

This has consequences that are easy to state and hard to keep in mind:

- It will be **most confident where the training material was thickest**, which is not the same as where the truth is clearest.
- It will **smooth over disagreement**, because an average of conflicting sources is a moderate-sounding blend of them.
- It will **reproduce the shape of its inputs**, including their blind spots, in proportion to how common those inputs were.
- It has **no stake** in whether it is right. It returns the same fluent tone for a true answer and a fabricated one.

## Holding one responsibly

**Read it as a claim, not a result.** Every generated artifact arrives with an implied assertion: *this is accurate, this is good, this is what you wanted.* Your job is to check the assertion, not to admire the fluency.

**Find the load-bearing parts.** In any output, a few specific things are doing the real work — a number, a name, a causal claim, a design decision. Verify those directly against something that is not another generation.

**Notice what it did not question.** The instrument answered the prompt you gave. If the prompt contained a bad assumption, the answer inherits it, confidently.

**Keep authorship.** If you pass the artifact on, you are the author of it now. "The model said so" is not a defence you would accept from anyone else.

## Where this note is going

The Laboratory work suggests that the *variance* between generations is itself informative — the places where ten runs of the same prompt disagree are often exactly the places where the instrument has the least real grounding. If that holds up, "run it several times and read the disagreement" becomes a practical literacy technique, not just a curiosity. That is the current thread.
