*The founding argument of the School, stated plainly so the rest can be built on it.*

## The claim

> The more powerful the instrument becomes, the more important the person using it becomes.

This runs against the intuition that powerful tools make the user matter less. A calculator, the story goes, means you no longer need arithmetic; a good enough camera means you no longer need to see. Extend the line and the person eventually disappears, replaced by the machine that does their job.

The line is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why.

## What a powerful instrument actually removes

A powerful instrument removes *effort*, not *judgement*. It collapses the distance between intention and result. What used to take a week of labour takes an afternoon; what took an afternoon takes a sentence.

When effort is expensive, effort is the bottleneck, and skill means being able to afford the effort — to draft the thing at all. When effort is cheap, the bottleneck moves. It moves to the decisions that effort used to postpone: what to make, whether it is any good, whether it is true, whether it should exist. Those decisions were always there. They were just hidden behind the work.

## The bottleneck moves to the person

So the instrument does not make the person less important. It relocates their importance. It moves it from *production* to *direction and judgement*, and it does so while making it much easier not to notice.

This is the trap. A powerful instrument returns competent-looking results so quickly that it feels as though a decision has been made, when in fact a decision has only been *deferred* — to you, later, if you bother to look. The person who does not look has not escaped responsibility. They have just exercised it carelessly.

## Two failure modes

**Abdication.** You treat the instrument's output as an answer rather than a draft. You ship it. Whatever was wrong, unconsidered, or untrue in it is now wrong, unconsidered, or untrue in your name.

**Paralysis.** You distrust the instrument so completely that you refuse to use it, and you fall behind people who learned to direct it well. Refusing a tool is also a choice you are answerable for.

The School is interested in the narrow path between these: using the instrument fully, and staying the author of the result.

## What this asks of a creative engineer

- Know roughly how the instrument works, so its output does not look like magic.
- Treat every output as a draft with an implied claim attached — *this is true, this is good, this is worth your attention* — and check the claim.
- Keep a view of your own about what you are making and why, so the instrument has something to be directed *toward*.
- Accept that the result is yours. The instrument does not sign its work.

Everything else in the Library, the Practice, and the Laboratory is an attempt to make this concrete.
